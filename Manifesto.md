THE LOKI 97 STANDARD
L97S Manifesto — v0.1
Give the individual the computer back.

Personal computing should increase the agency of the individual using it.
A computer should be a tool that expands what its owner can understand, create, repair, explore, automate, and accomplish. Technology should serve the person using it—not quietly transfer authority over their hardware, information, work, or choices to the company that built it.
We believe technology can become easier without becoming less capable.
Simplicity should come from good design, not from removing power. Most people should be able to use a system without understanding its deepest layers, while those who want to go farther should be allowed to. Complexity may be optional. Capability should not be artificially withheld.
We believe technology can become connected without surrendering local ownership.
Cloud computing, synchronization, remote services, and shared infrastructure can provide real value. They should extend what a person's technology can do rather than become unnecessary points of dependency. When a capability can reasonably operate on hardware the user owns, that local capability should remain available to the user.
We believe ownership should mean something.
People should be able to use the hardware they own, retain access to the work they create, preserve their data, understand where it goes, and move it between systems when reasonably possible. Applications are tools. The user's work belongs to the user.
We believe integration should add capability, not manufacture dependency.
Products may become more powerful when they work together, but they should not be deliberately crippled when separated. A larger ecosystem should reward participation by adding value—not punish independence by removing capability.
We believe security should protect human authority, not replace it.
Strong security can require authentication, isolation, permissions, warnings, audit trails, and deliberate limits on automated systems. Those protections should exist to preserve the legitimate user's control of the system. Capability, authority, identity, and trust should remain deliberately distinct.
We believe privacy should be architectural.
A promise to protect information is weaker than designing systems that do not unnecessarily surrender that information in the first place. Local processing should be available where practical, and users should be able to understand when their information leaves their systems and why.
We believe automation and intelligence should increase human agency.
As computers become capable of interpreting intent and acting on a person's behalf, those systems should help people do more—not make their own actions less understandable. Intelligent automation should be inspectable where practical, and greater machine intelligence should result in greater human capability.
We believe compatibility matters because people's past work matters.
Progress should provide a path forward rather than demand that people abandon their existing files, software, hardware, workflows, and knowledge. New systems should strive to understand and work with what came before them while creating better ways to work in the future.
We believe payment should purchase value, not dependency.
Developers, infrastructure, support, hosting, compute, and continuing services have real costs and deserve sustainable business models. But locally delivered capability should not be artificially disabled merely to force continuing payment when no continuing service is necessary to provide it.
Above all, we believe the purpose of personal technology is to expand the capability of the person using it.
Technology exists to expand human capability.
L97S is not nostalgia for an older computer industry, nor a claim that the past was perfect. It is a commitment to carry forward a principle that personal computing has too often lost: the person at the keyboard is not merely a customer, an account, a source of data, or a recurring revenue opportunity. They are the reason the machine exists.
The Loki 97 Standard exists to turn that belief into a measurable engineering standard. The manifesto states the philosophy. The L97S specification and conformance tests define the requirements software must meet to carry the L97S mark.
Give the individual the computer back.
Simple for everyone. Powerful for anyone.
Why 97?
The name began as a joke about the capability and philosophy of personal computing in 1997—particularly the idea that software could be approachable while still placing extraordinary power in the hands of an individual. The joke became useful because it gave a name to a much larger idea.
L97S does not seek to recreate 1997. It seeks to carry forward the best principle behind that era of personal computing: give people capable tools, let curiosity be rewarded with deeper control, and remember who the computer is supposed to serve.
